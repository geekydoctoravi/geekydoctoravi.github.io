JREM 2.0 · Proposed conceptual framework

Joy, Regulation &
Generative Capacity

Three independent dimensions for understanding an experience in any social context. Joy, nervous-system regulation and the capacity to create or contribute can move together—but they do not have to.

1. Three independent axes

Do not collapse these into one “good-to-bad” ladder. Each axis answers a different question.

Axis A · Reward & meaning

Joy potential

How pleasurable, absorbing, satisfying or meaningful does this experience feel?

Low: flat, unrewarding, aversive
Middle: pleasant, comforting, worthwhile
High: delight, deep satisfaction, meaning

Axis B · Flexibility & recovery

Regulatory quality

Does this experience support a sense of safety, flexibility, recovery and choice—or leave you overwhelmed, shut down or depleted?

Low: dysregulated or depleted
Middle: settling or stabilizing
High: flexible, connected, resilient

Axis C · Agency & output

Generative capacity

Does the experience enable learning, problem-solving, creativity, contribution or purposeful action?

Low: stuck, passive, constrained
Middle: capable, steady, exploratory
High: flow, insight, collaboration, creation

5. Polyvagal theory as a fourth interpretive lens—not a score

Ventral-vagal-associated social engagement

Polyvagal Theory associates cues of safety with social engagement, connection and reciprocal communication. This can be relevant to safe peer contact, group belonging and flexible solo engagement.

Sympathetic mobilization

Mobilization supports action and defense. It can accompany anxiety or conflict, but also exercise, excitement, challenge and purposeful effort. Activation is not inherently unhealthy.

Dorsal-vagal-associated shutdown

The theory uses this term for a defensive immobilization or shutdown pattern. It may be a useful metaphor for some experiences of withdrawal or numbness, but it should not be inferred from a single behavior.

Use observable experience

Ask: Do I feel safe enough? Can I shift attention? Do I have choice? Can I connect or disengage? Am I recovering afterward? These are practical observations, not measurements of vagal activity.

Scientific caution: Polyvagal Theory is influential but debated, and some of its specific physiological and evolutionary claims remain contested. This model does not diagnose autonomic states or measure vagal tone. Treat the theory as one interpretive perspective alongside broader stress, emotion-regulation and social-connection research.

6. A practical decision sequence

If regulation is low

First consider safety and load: pause, reduce stimulation, change environment, move gently if helpful, or contact a trusted person. Do not assume that productivity or socializing is the right intervention.

If regulation is adequate but joy is low

Try a small source of interest, comfort, play or meaning. Some worthwhile activities are neutral rather than joyful; that is okay.

If joy is high but generativity is low

Enjoy the experience. Rest and play have value without needing to become achievement.

If all three are high

Notice the conditions that support it—autonomy, challenge, trust, energy and purpose—and protect them without chasing a permanent peak.

7. Model status and research agenda

JREM 2.0 is a proposed conceptual framework, not a validated psychometric instrument. To test it, researchers could gather repeated experience-sampling ratings for the three axes across Alone, Group and Peer contexts; examine whether the dimensions are empirically distinct; test reliability and predictive validity; and assess cultural and individual differences. Avoid treating the three scores as a single total.

Intellectual roots to explore: flow and optimal experience (Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi); social connection and autonomic regulation (Stephen W. Porges / Polyvagal Theory); collective effervescence (Émile Durkheim); emotion regulation and psychological flexibility research. These sources inform different components; they do not validate this combined model.
