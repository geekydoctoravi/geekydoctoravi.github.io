A working conceptual framework

Joy Regulation &
Engagement Model

JREM · 3 contexts × 3 levels
A map of how people relate to themselves, groups and individual peers—and how those interactions can become draining, restorative or generative.

How to use the map

Choose a cell. The levels describe patterns of interaction, not fixed personality types or a ranking of human worth. A person can move between them within one day—or one conversation.

2. Polyvagal-informed interpretation

The autonomic nervous system offers a second lens: not another set of joy levels, but a way to consider safety, mobilization and shutdown.

Ventral-vagal-associated safety

In Polyvagal Theory, cues of safety and social connection are associated with accessible social engagement: facial expression, vocal tone, listening and reciprocal contact.

JREM relevance: often supports Peer L2–L3 and Group L2–L3, but does not guarantee joy or creativity.

Sympathetic mobilization

Energy for action, defense, effort and movement. It may appear as anxiety or anger, but also as healthy challenge, excitement, sport and purposeful effort.

JREM relevance: can appear in Alone L3 exercise or focused work, as well as in reactive states. Activation alone is not “bad.”

Dorsal-vagal-associated shutdown

In the theory, this refers to a more immobilized, withdrawn or shut-down defensive response. Subjectively it may involve numbness, low energy or disconnection.

JREM relevance: may overlap with some draining experiences, but rumination is not automatically dorsal-vagal shutdown.

Mixed and changing states

Real nervous-system responses are complex. People can feel excitement and safety together, or be socially engaged while physiologically activated.

JREM relevance: read context, felt safety, flexibility and outcome—not just a label.

3. The movement principle

Notice the current state
→
Find a tolerable reset
→
Choose a fitting engagement
→
Reflect on the result

Do not force a jump from distress to peak performance. A small, appropriate shift may help: change posture, drink water, take a walk, reduce stimulation, contact a trusted person, or choose a manageable task. The right move depends on the person and situation.

4. Quality dimensions to assess

Useful outcomes

  • More flexibility and choice
  • Restored energy or steadier attention
  • Authentic connection and mutual respect
  • Meaningful creation or contribution
  • Ability to recover after stress

Warning signs

  • Repeated depletion without recovery
  • Loss of agency or blurred boundaries
  • Compulsive activity or avoidance
  • Social connection that depends on masking
  • “High joy” becoming pressure to perform
Important distinction: sadness is not automatically restorative, and overthinking is not a necessary lower stage before joy. Sadness can be meaningful and appropriate, but persistent distress deserves care. Rest, reflection and problem-solving are useful when they actually help—not because a model assigns them a higher rank.

5. What this model is—and is not

JREM is a proposed practical synthesis, not a validated psychological scale or a diagnostic instrument. Its nine cells are a vocabulary for reflection. Polyvagal Theory is influential but debated; specific claims about its evolutionary and physiological mechanisms remain contested. Use the framework as a hypothesis-generating map, not as a measurement of vagal tone or a clinical assessment.

Conceptual references for further study
Stephen W. Porges — Polyvagal Theory and the social engagement system; Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi — Flow; Émile Durkheim — collective effervescence; Aristotle — friendship and flourishing. These traditions inform different parts of the model and should not be treated as if they independently validate the JREM matrix.
